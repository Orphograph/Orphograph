How it works in three steps

Step 1

Drop a file at orphograph.com.

The browser computes a SHA-256 fingerprint locally; the file stays on the device.

Step 2

The office anchors only its fingerprint to Bitcoin.

Five independent OpenTimestamps calendars route the hash into a Bitcoin block, typically within the hour.

Step 3

You receive a receipt that verifies for as long as the chain exists.

The receipt can be checked by any third party against Bitcoin, using the open-source MIT verifier.

What the receipt proves, and what it does not

What the receipt proves What it does not prove
The exact bytes of the file existed by no later than the recorded Bitcoin block. Who created the file or who owns the rights to it.
No edit has been made to those bytes after the anchor; any change breaks the hash. That the content of the file is true, accurate, or original to any author.
The fingerprint is recorded on a public, append-only ledger no single party controls. That the file was created at any time earlier than the anchored block.
The receipt is independently verifiable, with or without the office still existing. A legal outcome in any jurisdiction — courts and adjusters reach their own conclusions.
