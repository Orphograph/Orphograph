Cryptographic proof a document existed at a date

For drafts, contracts, research notes, business plans — anything where the date matters and you want a third-party-verifiable anchor.

"Proof of existence" is a precise cryptographic claim: this exact file's bytes existed on or before this exact moment. It does not assert authorship. It does not assert identity. It does not assert that the document was true. What it asserts — and what no notary, lawyer, or email-timestamp can match — is a binding between content and time anchored in a public ledger that no single party controls.

For drafts, research notebooks, contracts, prior-art disclosures, internal memos, expert reports, and any document whose value depends on its date, that's often the only claim you actually need.

The mechanism, briefly

Your browser hashes the document with SHA-256 — a 32-byte fingerprint unique to those exact bytes. The hash is submitted to five OpenTimestamps calendars. Each calendar aggregates many hashes into a Merkle tree and writes the root of the tree to a Bitcoin transaction. The Bitcoin block's timestamp becomes the timestamp for every hash inside the tree.

You receive a small JSON receipt plus five binary .ots proof files. To verify later, anyone with the original document, the receipt, and the open-source verifier can confirm the timestamp directly against the Bitcoin chain. No Orphograph server is required in that path.

Privacy is structural, not a policy

Document contents stay on your machine. SHA-256 happens in the browser via WebCrypto. Only the hash is transmitted. The hash reveals nothing about what the document says — you could anchor a 200-page research report or a one-line confession and the hash looks the same: 64 hex characters. This is not a privacy promise we enforce with logs and policies; it's structural, because there is no upload path in the code.

Use cases worth the 30 seconds

  • Draft priority. You're writing a paper, a book, a business plan. Anchor each significant revision. If a dispute about timing arises, you can demonstrate which version existed when.
  • Contract delivery. The signed PDF the client received on Tuesday. Anchor it Tuesday morning. If the version is ever challenged, the hash settles which bytes are authentic.
  • Research notes. Lab notebooks, interview transcripts, anything where temporal sequence matters for credit or for cross-checking later claims.
  • Internal memos. Decisions documented on the day they were made. Useful in any after-the-fact review.
  • Expert reports. Establish that the report existed in its final form before any opposing party could have influenced its contents.

The honest limitations

We don't sell this as legal evidence. It's strong cryptographic evidence. In litigation, a digital-evidence specialist would want to see your receipt embedded in a broader chain-of-custody: who held the document, how it was stored, how it was produced. The receipt doesn't replace that workflow — it strengthens one specific link in it. Talk to a specialist if your situation is high-stakes.

FAQ

What does proof-of-existence actually demonstrate?

That a file with exactly these bytes existed by the anchored block time. It does not claim authorship or identity.

Can I anchor a PDF without revealing its contents?

Yes. SHA-256 is computed locally; only the 32-byte hash leaves your machine. The hash reveals nothing about the document.

How is this different from a notarized PDF?

A notary verifies identity and witnesses a signing. Orphograph proves bytes-existed-by-time. Different problems, different tools.

How long does the proof last?

As long as the Bitcoin chain. The receipt is self-contained and verifies without Orphograph.

What if I edit the document later?

The original anchor only matches the exact bytes you anchored. Anchor the revision separately if the new version matters too.
