For solo and small-firm counsel

One receipt covers a whole exhibit set. Show one exhibit later without showing the rest.

Hash a matter's exhibit folder in one step. After the timestamp confirms, a recipient can verify that a disclosed exhibit matches a leaf in that anchored set.

What this means for you

  • Exhibit bytes are hashed on your device rather than uploaded; the manifest sent for anchoring contains relative paths, file digests, sizes, and the root.
  • One successful folder receipt commits to the submitted exhibit manifest.
  • Verify one disclosed exhibit without supplying the other exhibit files.

How it works

Each exhibit gets a fingerprint — a 64-character code that changes if a single byte of the file changes. The fingerprints are computed on your own machine, not uploaded.

The fingerprints and relative paths are combined into one fingerprint for the whole folder. The anchoring request includes the manifest metadata and root, but not the exhibit bytes.

Once an OpenTimestamps proof is confirmed against Bitcoin, a verifier can test the disclosed exhibit and its recorded path against the receipt. The proof's Bitcoin block time is the supported time bound; a filing date entered by a user is not independently verified.

The matter folder, as an anchored unit

The suggested workflow is one folder submission per filing. The folder can contain documentary exhibits, photographs, screenshots, recordings, and other artifacts the firm intends to introduce. Successive successful submissions produce separate commitments; the receipt time does not independently establish when a filing occurred.

The desktop or browser tool computes a SHA-256 fingerprint of each artifact, binds the artifact's literal relative path into a leaf hash, and assembles the leaves into a Merkle tree whose root is submitted to the OpenTimestamps calendars. Artifact bytes are not uploaded, but relative paths, digests, sizes, and the root are manifest metadata and can themselves be sensitive. Rename or copy files to non-sensitive paths before anchoring when filenames are confidential.

Selective disclosure at trial

Opposing counsel moves to exclude a single text-message screenshot on grounds of post-hoc fabrication. The firm produces an inclusion proof that the screenshot was a leaf of the anchored exhibit folder no later than the attesting Bitcoin block. The other exhibit files are not needed to verify that proof. The receipt does not establish that the screenshot is genuine or that it was filed on a claimed date; it establishes the byte-and-path commitment's Bitcoin time bound.

What the office records

The folder receipt records the Merkle root, ordering rule, per-leaf relative paths, SHA-256 digests, byte sizes, and available OpenTimestamps attestations. An optional valid did:key signature can show that the corresponding private key signed the manifest. Orphograph does not establish who controls that key, validate bar status, or certify that the signer is counsel.

Questions

Where do my exhibits go?

The exhibit bytes remain on your machine. The service receives the folder manifest metadata and root, including literal relative paths, so use non-sensitive filenames or renamed copies where path disclosure matters.

Is this a chain-of-custody affidavit?

No. The receipt records that a particular byte sequence under a particular relative path was anchored by a particular Bitcoin block. The chain-of-custody narrative remains the province of counsel and the witness.

Can I anchor under sealed or confidential filenames?

The current folder format binds literal relative paths, so filenames are not confidential from the manifest recipient or service. Use renamed copies with non-sensitive relative paths before anchoring; keep the private mapping under your own controls.

Does this establish authenticity?

The receipt establishes that the disclosed artifact existed by the time of the recorded Bitcoin block and was a leaf of the anchored set. Authenticity in the evidentiary sense is a separate determination governed by the applicable rules of evidence and the court.
